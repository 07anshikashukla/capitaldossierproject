Remy Cointreau – Rhapsodized enough?

By Analyst | 5 min read

Some stocks generate value over years, delivering compounding that more than rewards the patience of investors.

There is comfort in legacy. When a company has been around for a better part of two centuries, commanding a refined niche, it’s almost instinctive to label it as “safe.” Analysts insist on prospective value creation, and over time, the label becomes self-reinforcing.

Rémy Cointreau is one such company. Known for its high-end cognac portfolio, including Rémy Martin and ultra-luxury offerings like Louis XIII, the business stews comfortably in the “premiumization” narrative - the idea that consumers will continue to trade up to better. Nonetheless compelling but investing is not only about rhapsodized narratives.

For years, the investment case around Rémy Cointreau has remained largely unchanged:

  • Strong brand equity
  • Pricing power
  • Exposure to affluent consumers
  • Premiumisation

These are not incorrect. In fact, they are precisely what make it a worthy investment.

But the market doesn’t reward goodness in isolation. It rewards timing, growth, and expectations. And that’s where the cracks in the story appear.

Since Covid, the company’s financial performance has been far less reassuring than what appears to be the optimism behind some analyst reports categorising it as a value story:

  • Growth has stagnated, with revenues barely budging, so much so the company had to relinquish its 2030 sales growth ambitions
  • Operating margins have come under pressure
  • Key markets like China and the United States - have weakened
  • Downtrading and demand normalization have weighed on performance

None of this suggests a broken business neither a smooth compounding story right now.

Legacy premium names often benefit from a bias, because of a robust brand, analysts often insist on the benefit of the doubt longer than they should.

Any weakness is assumed to be a turnaround waiting to happen. The arguments are always the same, “Demand will improve”, “Premiumization is a long-term play”, “Hiccups not setbacks.”

But these assumptions cannot just remain an outlined risk to an otherwise “engrossing” case.

Introduce me to two kinds of investors and the more patient one is always an institutional investor. The long horizons, diversified portfolios, and the ability to absorb periods of underperformance makes them patient and seasoned.

But what about the other? The retail investor?

When a stock continues to be favourable on street despite weak fundamentals, time often becomes a hidden cost arising from mismatch of expectations and performance. In such cases, it becomes rather pertinent to address if the small investors can ride the wave of unfettered optimism when jettison is the better option.

Rémy Cointreau’s strength lies heavily in a specific segment, and a limited set of geographies.

That concentration matters.

  • China demand has been volatile
  • U.S. consumption cycles have been sputtered
  • Regulatory and macro factors continue to affect the pulse of the business more than any factor

What appears as a wide moat may, in practice, be more dependent on external conditions than the narrative suggests.

Most bullish assumptions are built around the expectations of ‘normalisation’. But normalisation is not guaranteed. During 2020-2026, there have been some favourable arguments in the hopes of evolving consumer preferences, changing global demand cycles, inclination towards different categories falling in and out of favor.

But this story in particular has quietly become a multi-year waiting game.

This is not a bearish call on Rémy Cointreau. It remains a high-quality company with strong brands and long-term potential. But potential doesn’t always translate to a good stock.

For investors, especially retail investors, the job is not to buy comfort.

It is to question it.
